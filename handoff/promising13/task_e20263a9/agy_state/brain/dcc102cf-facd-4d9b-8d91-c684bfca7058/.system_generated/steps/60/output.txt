In graph theory, there is no single constant or specific formula relating the **Roman domination number** ($\gamma_R(G)$) directly to the **radius** ($rad(G)$) of a graph[1][2]. Instead, researchers often investigate the relationship between these two parameters to establish bounds or compare them across different classes of graphs[1][2].

### Key Concepts
*   **Roman Domination Number ($\gamma_R(G)$):** The minimum weight of a Roman dominating function, which is a labeling $f: V \to \{0, 1, 2\}$ such that every vertex labeled $0$ is adjacent to at least one vertex labeled $2$[3][4].
*   **Radius of a Graph ($rad(G)$):** The minimum eccentricity among all vertices in the graph[3][5]. The eccentricity of a vertex is the maximum distance from that vertex to any other vertex in the graph[3][5].

### Relationship in Literature
*   **Comparison Studies:** Studies often compare the Roman domination number with the radius, total domination number, and other distance-based parameters[1][2]. For instance, some research has analyzed these relationships specifically for certain graph classes, such as **trapezoid graphs**, where it has been noted in specific contexts that the Roman domination number can be greater than the radius[1][2].
*   **Bounds:** While a universal equality does not exist, researchers frequently develop lower and upper bounds for the Roman domination number that incorporate structural properties like the radius, diameter, or degree sequences of the graph[6][7][8].
*   **Distance Variants:** There are extensions of the concept, such as **global $k$-distance Roman domination**, which explicitly involve the distance between vertices (the basis for the definition of the radius) to define how vertices are "dominated"[9][5]. In these cases, the "radius" of the domination range ($k$) is a parameter defined within the problem itself[9].

If you are looking for a specific theorem or bound for a particular type of graph (e.g., trees, grid graphs, or cycles), the relationship is usually highly dependent on the graph's structure[10][11].

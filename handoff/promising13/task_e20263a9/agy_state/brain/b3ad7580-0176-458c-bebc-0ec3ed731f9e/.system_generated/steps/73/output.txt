In graph theory, the relationship between the **radius** ($\text{rad}(G)$) and the **Roman domination number** ($\gamma_R(G)$) is a subject of study involving various bounds and extremal properties[1][2].

### Key Concepts
*   **Roman Domination Number ($\gamma_R(G)$):** The minimum weight of a function $f: V \to \{0, 1, 2\}$ such that every vertex labeled $0$ is adjacent to at least one vertex labeled $2$[1][3][4].
*   **Radius ($\text{rad}(G)$):** The minimum eccentricity among all vertices in a graph, where eccentricity is the maximum distance from a vertex to any other vertex in the graph[1][5][6].

### Relationships and Bounds
Research comparing these parameters often focuses on how the structural constraints of a graph (such as its radius) influence the efficiency of its Roman dominating functions[2]. 

*   **General Bounds:** While there is no single universal "radius-Roman" formula, various studies explore bounds for specific graph classes. For instance, literature exists comparing the Roman domination number to other graph invariants like the independence number, domination number, and radius[7][2].
*   **Extremal Problems:** Many papers investigate "extremal graphs"—graphs that reach the upper or lower limits of these bounds[8][9]. Researchers have looked into how the radius serves as a parameter in these inequalities, particularly in trees or specific graph structures[1][10].
*   **Contextual Bounds:** 
    *   For many classes of graphs, the Roman domination number is bounded by expressions involving the order ($n$) and the minimum degree ($\delta$) or maximum degree ($\Delta$)[11][12][13][14].
    *   Specific research has noted that if the radius of a simple, connected graph equals its independence number, certain bounds on domination-related parameters can be established[10].

### Further Reading
If you are looking for specific theorems or proofs:
*   **Surveys on Roman Domination:** Papers by authors such as Chellali, Jafari Rad, Sheikholeslami, and Volkmann provide comprehensive surveys on the various bounds and properties of Roman domination[15][16].
*   **Comparative Studies:** Research papers specifically titled along the lines of "Comparison of Roman domination number... with radius of a graph" often provide the direct inequalities you may be seeking for specific graph types (e.g., trapezoid graphs or trees)[2].
